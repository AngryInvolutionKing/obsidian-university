### BCI: From Neural Signals to Control Systems

What if you could type a message, steer a wheelchair, or play a video game without moving a muscle? This is not science fiction. It is the central promise of the brain–computer interface (BCI), which translates electrical brain activity into commands for machines. Over the past decade, BCIs have moved from laboratory curiosities to systems that are changing lives, with China becoming an active contributor to this rapidly evolving field.

The Language of the Brain

Every action you take — reaching for a cup, speaking a word, even imagining a movement — begins as a pattern of electrical signals in the brain. Billions of neurons communicate with one another through brief voltage changes called action potentials. When large populations of neurons fire together, they produce measurable patterns of electrical activity. It is these patterns that BCI systems aim to capture and interpret.

But reading the brain’s electrical language is not straightforward. Neural signals are extraordinarily small — often measured in microvolts (millionths of a volt) — and they are easily contaminated by noise from muscle movements, heartbeats, and the surrounding environment. One of the core challenges in BCI engineering is extracting meaningful signals from this noisy background, a process known as signal acquisition.

Three Windows into the Brain

BCI systems differ most fundamentally in how they access neural signals, and researchers generally classify them into three categories based on invasiveness. Non-invasive systems, the most common type, place sensors on the surface of the scalp. The best-known technique is electroencephalography (EEG), which uses an array of small electrodes to detect the brain’s electrical fields through the skull. EEG is safe, portable, and relatively inexpensive, which makes it attractive for applications ranging from rehabilitation to consumer electronics. However, because signals must pass through the skull and scalp, EEG recordings have limited spatial resolution — they capture the general region of activity rather than the behaviour of individual neurons.

At the other extreme, invasive BCIs involve surgically implanting microelectrode arrays in brain tissue. These devices record individual neurons with high precision, enabling finer control. During 2025 clinical trials led by the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Huashan Hospital, ultra-thin flexible electrodes about one-hundredth the diameter of a human hair were implanted in participants with severe motor impairment. After training, participants controlled computer tasks or powered wheelchairs by thought. The system’s end-to-end delay fell below 100 milliseconds, supporting responsive control in interactive tasks.

Between these two extremes lie semi-invasive approaches, which place electrodes on the surface of the brain (beneath the skull) but do not penetrate brain tissue. This method, called electrocorticography (ECoG), offers a compromise: better signal quality than EEG, with lower surgical risk than fully invasive implants.

From Raw Signals to Useful Commands

Capturing neural signals is only the first step. The raw data must then be processed and translated into commands — a stage known as signal decoding. Modern BCI systems rely heavily on algorithms, often powered by machine learning, that learn to recognise which patterns of brain activity correspond to which intended actions. For example, a decoding algorithm might learn that a specific pattern in the motor cortex corresponds to the user’s intention to move a cursor to the left.

Decoding is particularly challenging because the brain is not static. Neural patterns can shift over time due to fatigue, learning, or changes in attention — a problem engineers call signal drift. To address this, researchers at the Chinese Academy of Sciences developed an “online recalibration” technology that adjusts the system’s parameters in real time during daily activities, rather than requiring the user to stop and recalibrate manually. This self-adapting capability is considered a significant step toward making BCIs practical for everyday use.

Closing the Loop

The most advanced BCI systems do more than send commands in one direction. They also return information to the user, creating what engineers call a closed-loop system. In a closed-loop BCI, the user receives feedback — visual, auditory, or even tactile — that confirms whether the intended action was successfully carried out. This feedback allows the user to adjust their mental strategy in real time, improving accuracy and speed with practice.

Closed-loop design is essential for applications where precision matters, such as controlling a robotic arm to grasp an object or navigating a wheelchair through a crowded space. It also plays a critical role in rehabilitation: when a stroke patient attempts to move a paralysed hand and a BCI-driven device responds by assisting that movement, the simultaneous brain effort and physical feedback can promote neuroplasticity — the brain’s ability to reorganise itself by forming new neural connections.

A Field in Motion

BCI technology is advancing rapidly. In March 2026, China approved the NEO hand-movement compensation system, developed by Neuracle and Tsinghua University, as the first implantable BCI medical device cleared for marketing. Tianjin University has pushed steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP) command sets beyond 200 inputs. Progress also raises ethical questions: Who owns neural data? How can access remain equitable? What happens as human cognition and machine control become more closely connected?

These are not distant questions. They are engineering challenges — and they belong to your generation.

### Thinking Like an Engineer

Read two short reports on real-world BCI and biomedical devices. Consider how these technologies affect the people who actually use them — their comfort, safety, and daily experience.

Report 1

Invasive BCI implants require brain surgery to place electrodes inside or on the surface of the brain. While these devices achieve high decoding accuracy, patients face risks including infection, tissue scarring, and gradual electrode degradation. The implanted hardware typically cannot be upgraded without another surgery. Some systems also require the patient to wear an external transmitter on the head, which must be precisely positioned each time and may cause skin irritation over months of use. Current battery-powered implants need periodic recharging — in some designs, the patient must sit still near a wireless charging pad for up to an hour each day.

Report 2

Non-invasive EEG-based rehabilitation systems avoid surgery entirely, but they come with their own user-experience challenges. Patients must wear electrode caps that require conductive gel applied to each sensor point — a messy, time-consuming process that can take 20–40 minutes before each session. The gel dries out during use, degrading signal quality over longer sessions. For elderly stroke patients with limited mobility, sitting upright in a rigid position while wearing the cap can be physically exhausting. Some patients report headaches or scalp discomfort after repeated sessions, and the overall setup complexity means a trained operator must be present throughout.
