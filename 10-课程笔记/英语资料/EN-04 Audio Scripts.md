### Unit 1


Audio 1: Engineering Conversations | Peers’ Lab Experiences

Lin Jing asks a senior student Wang Hao for help with a temperature-sensor lab report.

Lin Jing: Hey, Wang Hao! Do you have a minute? I'm really struggling with my sensor lab report.

Wang Hao: Sure, what's up?

Lin Jing: Well, we did this temperature sensor experiment last week, and I'm totally confused. My data is all over the place! Look at these numbers—they're supposed to be measuring room temperature, but the readings jump from 22 degrees to 24 degrees and back again every few seconds. I don't know if my sensor is broken or if I did something wrong.

Wang Hao: Ah, the classic first-year sensor lab problem! Don't worry, your sensor probably isn't broken. What you're seeing is called noise.

Lin Jing: Noise? You mean like... sound?

Wang Hao: No, not that kind of noise. In engineering, noise means random variations in your measurements. Think of it like static on a radio—it's interference that makes your signal less clear.

Lin Jing: Oh! So it's normal to have these fluctuations?

Wang Hao: Exactly. All sensors have some noise. It can come from different sources. For example, electromagnetic interference from nearby equipment—like if someone's phone is too close to your sensor. Or temperature drift—if the room temperature changes slightly while you're measuring. Even the sensor itself generates some internal noise.

Lin Jing: That makes sense, but how do I explain this in my report? My professor wants us to analyze our errors, and I don't even know where to start.

Wang Hao: Okay, here's what you need to do. First, in your error analysis section, identify the possible sources of noise in your experiment. Ask yourself: What could have affected my measurements? Was the lab door opening and closing? Were there computers running nearby? Write these down.

Lin Jing: Got it. What else?

Wang Hao: Second, look at your data statistically. Calculate the average of all your readings—that's probably close to the true temperature. Then look at how much the readings vary. If most of your data points are between 22 and 24 degrees, you can say your measurement is "23 degrees plus or minus 1 degree."

Lin Jing: Oh! So I'm not supposed to just pick one number?

Wang Hao: Exactly! Real measurements always have uncertainty. That's totally normal in engineering.

Lin Jing: Okay, that helps a lot. But there's another thing—the TA mentioned something about calibration, and I have no idea what that means.

Wang Hao: Calibration is super important. It's basically making sure your sensor is reading accurately. Think of it like checking if a scale is correct by weighing something you know is exactly one kilogram.

Lin Jing: So... you compare your sensor to something you know is right?

Wang Hao: Exactly! For your temperature sensor, you might compare it to a high-quality reference thermometer. If your sensor reads 21 degrees but the reference thermometer reads 22 degrees, you know your sensor is off by one degree. Then you can adjust all your readings.

Lin Jing: Wait, so I should have calibrated my sensor before the experiment?

Wang Hao: Ideally, yes. But if you didn't, just mention it in your report. You can say "This experiment did not include a calibration step, which may have contributed to measurement uncertainty." Professors like it when you recognize the limitations of your experiment.

Lin Jing: Oh, that's a relief! I thought I'd totally messed up.

Wang Hao: Not at all! Look, here's a quick structure for your error analysis section. First, describe what errors you observed—like the fluctuations you mentioned. Second, explain possible sources of those errors—noise from interference, lack of calibration, whatever. Third, suggest how you could improve the experiment next time—maybe calibrate first, or take more measurements to average out the noise.

Lin Jing: That's really clear! Can I ask one more thing? How many measurements should I have taken? I only did about ten readings.

Wang Hao: Ten is probably okay for a basic lab, but more is always better. If you're seeing a lot of noise, taking 20 or 30 measurements and averaging them gives you a more reliable result. It's like taking multiple photos and choosing the clearest one.

Lin Jing: That makes so much sense! I wish someone had explained this before the lab.

Wang Hao: Yeah, sensor labs can be confusing at first. But once you understand noise and calibration, everything else gets easier. Trust me, by your third year, this will be second nature.

Lin Jing: I hope so! Thanks so much, Wang Hao. You just saved my weekend—I was planning to spend the whole time staring at my data!

Wang Hao: No problem! That's what senior students are for. Good luck with your report!

### Audio 2: Engineering Decision | Sensor Choice for an Underwater Robot

Su Fei and Wang Hao choose sensors for an underwater-robot competition.

Su Fei: Hi Wang Hao! I’m excited about our underwater robot project, but we need to choose the sensors soon. Our club only has about 3,000 yuan.

Wang Hao: I’ve checked a few options. I think we should use sonar. It works in almost any water condition.

Su Fei: How much does sonar cost?

Wang Hao: A basic system is about 1,800 yuan. It can detect obstacles up to 30 meters away, even in muddy water.

Su Fei: That’s more than half our budget. What about cameras? Aren’t they cheaper?

Wang Hao: Yes, an underwater camera is about 450 yuan. But the competition is in a lake, and lake water is often turbid. Cameras may not see clearly.

Su Fei: But if we spend 1,800 yuan on sonar, we’ll only have about 1,200 yuan left for motors, batteries, and the robot body.

Wang Hao: I understand. But if the robot cannot detect obstacles in muddy water, it may crash. Last year, after rain, three teams’ robots crashed because their cameras couldn’t see clearly.

Su Fei: Really? Could we test the robot in the lake before the competition?

Wang Hao: No. The rules only allow testing in our university pool, and the pool water is very clean. So camera testing there won’t show the real problem.

Su Fei: What if we buy a camera first and upgrade to sonar later?

Wang Hao: The competition is only two months away. If we decide too late, we won’t have enough time to integrate sonar and test it properly.

Su Fei: Is there a cheaper sonar option?

Wang Hao: Yes. I found a simpler sonar for about 900 yuan. Its range is 15 meters, but that should be enough for a small competition area.

Su Fei: That sounds much better. Then we still have about 2,100 yuan left.

Wang Hao: Exactly. We could use both sensors: 900 yuan for sonar and 450 yuan for a camera. That’s 1,350 yuan total, leaving 1,650 yuan for the rest.

Su Fei: Why use both?

Wang Hao: They complement each other. Sonar measures distance in muddy water, while the camera gives visual details in clear water, like markers or colored objects.

Su Fei: So it’s a backup system. If one sensor has a problem, the other can still help.

Wang Hao: Exactly. That’s sensor fusion — using multiple sensors together to make the system more reliable.

Su Fei: Alright, I support the dual-sensor plan. But we must control the remaining 1,650 yuan carefully.

Wang Hao: Agreed. Let’s make a detailed budget for motors, batteries, and housing. I can handle the basic sensor-fusion programming.

Su Fei: So our final decision is: 900 yuan for basic sonar plus 450 yuan for an underwater camera. That leaves 1,650 yuan for the rest.

Wang Hao: Exactly. This way, our robot can work in both clear and turbid water. Let’s present the plan to our advisor tomorrow.

Wang Hao, Lin Jing, and Su Fei are having a project-team meeting about a smart-factory systems project.

Wang Hao: Okay, team, we need to fix the main problem in our Smart Factory Systems project. The dashboard looks good, but the numbers don’t match what we see in the workshop.

Lin Jing: Which data point is causing the biggest mismatch?

Wang Hao: OEE. The robot looks busy, but the dashboard marks it as “idle.” The sensors send data, but the system interpretation seems wrong.

Su Fei: That may be a data definition problem. Did we define “running,” “idle,” and “downtime” consistently across the CNC machine, conveyor, and robot cell?

Wang Hao: Not really. We used the default tags from the gateway. I assumed the data already had a clear meaning.

Lin Jing: Then that’s our first task. In smart manufacturing, data quality is part of production quality. If definitions are inconsistent, OEE and scheduling decisions will be unreliable.

Su Fei: Before AI, we need a shared tag dictionary. “Idle” must mean the same thing across machines, or be clearly defined for each machine type.

Wang Hao: Agreed. But we can’t rebuild the whole system before the deadline.

Lin Jing: Then we narrow the scope: one line, one bottleneck, one question. Let’s choose the robot cell and ask, “Why is delivery late?”

Su Fei: The pipeline should be: sensor data, gateway, storage, dashboard, and validation. We compare the dashboard status with direct workshop observation.

Wang Hao: Good. What about predictive maintenance? The professor expects us to include it.

Lin Jing: We include it as a future extension. AI only works if we trust the timestamps, machine status, and failure labels. Otherwise, the model predicts nonsense.

Su Fei: So our message is: no clean data, no useful AI.

Wang Hao: Exactly. Honest and professional.

Lin Jing: We should also mention edge computing. For low-latency decisions, like anomaly alarms, some processing should happen at the edge, not only in the cloud.

Su Fei: And cybersecurity. Connected lines need segmentation, authentication, and audit trails, especially for anything that changes robot programs or production recipes.

Wang Hao: That links to our made-to-order demo. If the product variant changes, the line must load the correct program. One wrong version could cause a safety or quality problem.

Lin Jing: Right. That’s why the digital thread matters. Order ID, design version, process parameters, robot program, and quality records should stay connected.

Su Fei: So our revised logic is: define the data, build one reliable pipeline, validate OEE, add edge alarms and cybersecurity, then discuss predictive maintenance.

Wang Hao: Good. Lin Jing, please create the tag dictionary and OEE definitions.

Lin Jing: Yes. I’ll define “running,” “idle,” “downtime,” and “alarm state” for the robot cell.

Wang Hao: Su Fei, can you handle the dashboard and pipeline diagram?

Su Fei: Sure. I’ll show the flow from sensor to gateway, storage, dashboard, and validation, and mark where edge processing happens.

Wang Hao: I’ll cover cybersecurity and the digital thread.

Lin Jing: This is stronger than simply saying, “We connected machines and used AI.” Now we’re showing engineering reasoning.

Su Fei: And if the dashboard still lies, we won’t blame the sensor first.

Wang Hao: Right. We’ll check the meaning of the data first. Okay, team — one bottleneck, one pipeline, clean definitions, then AI. Let’s revise the slides tonight.

Audio 2: Engineering Decision | Speed Versus Reliability in a Smart-Factory Upgrade

Wang Hao, Zhang Chen, and Lin Jing are having a project discussion about upgrading a factory to a smart manufacturing system.

Wang Hao: Zhang Chen, look — if we install sensors on every machine and connect everything to the cloud, we can finally get real-time dashboards for the whole workshop. Predictive maintenance, automatic scheduling… the full smart-factory package.

Zhang Chen: Connecting everything is easy to say. But our last demo already showed the problem: the “idle” tag was wrong half the time. If the status signals are messy, your real-time scheduling will be garbage. You’ll automate confusion.

Wang Hao: That’s why we should collect more data. More sensors mean more truth.

Lin Jing: Or more noise. Before “more data,” do we even agree on definitions? What exactly counts as downtime? What is a changeover? If each team labels events differently, we can’t build a reliable digital thread.

Wang Hao: Fine, definitions matter. But the factory wants results now. They’re losing orders because made-to-order variants are increasing. If we don’t upgrade fast, they’ll fall behind.

Zhang Chen: Fast upgrades create new risks. If you connect legacy machines, you expand the cybersecurity attack surface. One weak password, one unpatched gateway — and someone could change a robot program or a production recipe.

Wang Hao: You mean… like a real attack?

Zhang Chen: Or a “friendly mistake.” A technician loads the wrong version of the recipe. The line produces 500 units with the wrong component before anyone notices. Without traceability, you won’t even know which customers got which batch.

Lin Jing: And there’s also the human side. If the system constantly reschedules tasks, operators may stop trusting it. Or they follow the dashboard blindly and lose situational awareness. Smart factories need human-in-the-loop design, not just automation.

Wang Hao: So what do we do — nothing? We can’t just stay manual.

Zhang Chen: Not nothing — prioritize. Start with one bottleneck station. Build a clean data pipeline at the edge: accurate status, timestamps, and quality checks. Then expand. And segment the network before connecting everything.

Lin Jing: Exactly. The goal isn’t “maximum tech.” The goal is trustworthy decisions. A smaller system that works is better than a bigger system that lies.

Wang Hao: Okay… so the dilemma is speed versus reliability. If we move fast, we might break trust and safety. If we move slow, we might lose competitiveness.

Audio 1: Engineering Conversations | Starting a Scoping Review on Embodied AI

Lin Jing asks Zhang Chen for help planning a scoping review on embodied AI.

Lin Jing: Zhang Chen, do you have a minute? I’m really struggling with my Embodied AI assignment. The teacher asked us to do a scoping review of the literature, but honestly, I have never done one before. I don’t even know where to start.

Zhang Chen: A scoping review? I know what you mean. It sounds complicated at first. What part is confusing you the most?

Lin Jing: Almost everything. Should I just search “embodied AI” online and read whatever I find? There are so many articles, conference papers, and reports. I don’t know which ones are actually useful.

Zhang Chen: I had the same problem last semester. The first step is not collecting as many papers as possible. You need to decide what question you want to explore. For example, are you interested in how robots perceive the world, how AI models control robots, or how embodied AI is applied in real situations?

Lin Jing: So the research question helps me decide what information I need.

Zhang Chen: Exactly. After that, you can search more systematically. University databases, academic journals, and conference papers are usually better starting points than random websites.

Lin Jing: But how do I know whether a paper is reliable? Some articles look very professional.

Zhang Chen: Good question. Look at several things: who published it, whether it provides evidence, whether the methods are clearly explained, and whether it is closely related to your topic. A paper about robot hardware may not help much if your focus is human–robot interaction.

Lin Jing: That makes sense. Another problem is the final report. Do I just summarize every paper one by one?

Zhang Chen: No, that would become a long list. A good review should show patterns. You need to explain what researchers are focusing on, what approaches they use, and what challenges remain.

Lin Jing: Can I use AI tools to help? I was thinking about asking AI to summarize papers for me.

Zhang Chen: AI can be useful, but we need to use it carefully. It can help generate keywords, organize notes, or explain difficult concepts. But we still need to check the original papers and make our own judgments.

Lin Jing: So AI can help me explore the field, but it cannot replace my understanding.

Zhang Chen: Exactly. Especially for an emerging field like embodied AI, engineers need to learn how to find information, evaluate evidence, and build their own understanding.

Lin Jing: I think I know how to begin now. First, define my question, then search and evaluate sources, and finally organize the major ideas.

Zhang Chen: That’s the right approach. Learning a new technology is not only about knowing the answers. It is also about knowing how to find reliable answers.

Audio 1: Engineering Conversations | What Makes Materials Smart

A materials science lecture. Professor Li is explaining self-healing concrete. Two students, Zhang Chen and Su Fei, are in the audience.

Professor Li: …so to summarise, when micro-cracks appear in the concrete, the embedded capsules rupture and release a healing agent — typically a bacterial solution or epoxy resin — that fills and seals the crack automatically. Now, can anyone tell me why this matters for infrastructure? Yes — Zhang Chen?

Zhang Chen: Is it because repairing bridges and highways manually is extremely expensive? If the material can fix itself, you save on maintenance over the structure’s entire lifespan.

Professor Li: Exactly. The lifecycle cost drops significantly. Some estimates suggest a 30 to 40 percent reduction in maintenance spending over fifty years. And it’s not just about money — think about the disruption. Every time you close a lane on a highway to repair a crack, you cause traffic delays, carbon emissions from idling vehicles, and safety risks for the repair crew. A self-healing material avoids all of that. Now, here’s something I’d like you all to think about. If the healing agent runs out — say, after several crack–heal cycles — what happens to the structure?

Su Fei: Professor Li, I have a question about that. Does the material become weaker after the capsules are used up? Like, does it go back to being ordinary concrete?

Professor Li: That’s a very sharp question, Su Fei. The short answer is: yes, once the capsules are depleted, the self-healing function is gone. The concrete itself is still there, but it can no longer repair new cracks. This is one of the biggest challenges researchers are working on right now — how to make the healing mechanism renewable. Does anyone have thoughts on how we might solve this?

Zhang Chen: I read a paper last week about using vascular networks instead of capsules — like tiny tubes running through the concrete that can be refilled with healing agent from outside. Is that a realistic solution?

Professor Li: Good, you’ve been reading ahead. Vascular systems are promising, but they add complexity. The tubes can clog over time. They can weaken the concrete if they’re too large in diameter. And pumping healing agent into a bridge deck in the middle of a highway is not exactly convenient. So there’s a real engineering trade-off: better healing capacity versus added structural risk and maintenance difficulty.

Su Fei: So it’s kind of like… the more “smart” you make a material, the more things can go wrong with it?

Professor Li: That’s a really perceptive way to put it. In engineering, we call this the complexity–reliability trade-off. Every additional function you build into a material is another potential failure mode. A simple concrete beam has very few ways to fail — it either holds the load or it doesn’t. But a self-healing concrete with vascular networks? Now you have to worry about capsule depletion, tube clogging, chemical degradation of the healing agent, temperature sensitivity — the list goes on.

Zhang Chen: Does that mean engineers sometimes choose the simpler material on purpose, even if the smart option exists?

Professor Li: Absolutely. And this is something I really want you to remember. In real-world engineering, the best material is not always the most advanced one. It’s the one that best balances performance, cost, reliability, and maintainability for a specific application. A self-healing concrete might be perfect for an undersea tunnel where you can’t easily send repair crews. But for a simple car park, ordinary concrete with regular inspection might be the smarter choice — “smart” in the engineering sense, not the materials science sense.

Su Fei: That actually makes a lot of sense. I think I was assuming that newer always means better.

Professor Li: That’s a very common assumption, and a good one to let go of early in your career. Alright, speaking of smart materials that respond to their environment — let’s move on to shape-memory alloys. These are metals that can actually remember their original shape…

Audio 2: Engineering Decision | Who Pays for Year Twenty?

A project review meeting. Zhang Chen, Lin Jing, and their supervisor, Professor Wang, are reviewing the preliminary design for a landmark pedestrian bridge.

Professor Wang: The client wants a landmark bridge — glass deck, CFRP cables, single pylon, very dramatic. Zhang Chen, walk me through the structural concept.

Zhang Chen: I’m proposing CFRP cables instead of steel. They’re lighter, corrosion-free, and allow us to reduce the pylon size significantly. The visual impact is strong.

Professor Wang: The performance is excellent. But let me ask you something. Who inspects those cables in Year 15? Every maintenance crew in China knows how to handle steel cables. CFRP requires specialist equipment and trained technicians. How many of those exist in a third-tier city?

Zhang Chen: I hadn’t thought about that. I was focused on structural performance.

Professor Wang: That’s the mistake I see young engineers make. You design for Day One — the ribbon cutting. But a bridge has to survive Day 7,000. And Day 7,000 is a Tuesday morning when a local worker needs to replace a component, and the manual says “contact the original manufacturer in Shenzhen.” Lin Jing, any question?

Lin Jing: Professor Wang, I inalizi something. Same problem with the glass deck. I ran the numbers — if panels are replaced every eight years, the maintenance cost over fifty years reaches nearly 40 percent of the original construction budget. And every replacement means closing the bridge.

Professor Wang: So what’s the solution? Do we make the bridge smarter?

Lin Jing: We could embed structural health monitoring — fibre-optic sensors feeding a digital twin. Instead of expensive scheduled inspections, the system tells you exactly when and where to intervene. You shift from time-based maintenance to condition-based maintenance.

Zhang Chen: But the monitoring system itself needs maintenance. Sensors fail. Software needs updating. Are we solving a problem or replacing it with a different one?

Professor Wang: Here’s what I’ve learned in fifteen years. Design in layers. The base structure must be safe and maintainable using conventional methods — no technology required. The smart layer sits on top as an enhancement, not a dependency. If every sensor fails tomorrow, the bridge must still be inspectable by a regular crew with standard tools. I call it the Tuesday Morning Test: if your design only works when the right specialist is available, it will fail in the real world.

Lin Jing: So smart systems make maintenance better, but the structure shouldn’t depend on them to be safe.

Professor Wang: Exactly. And one more thing. When you present a design, show the client the fifty-year total cost — construction plus inspection plus repair plus replacement. A slightly less dramatic design that saves the city 30 million RMB in maintenance over five decades — that’s money for schools and hospitals. That’s engineering responsibility. It’s not always about building the most impressive thing. Sometimes it’s about building the most honest thing.

Audio 1: Engineering Conversations | Balancing Entertainment and Professionalism

During the office hour, Zhang Chen asks Professor Chen for advice on a presentation.

Zhang Chen: Professor Chen, do you have a few minutes? I’m preparing our marine resource and environment presentation, and I’m not sure how to balance professionalism and audience engagement.

Professor Chen: Of course, Zhang Chen. What exactly are you worried about?

Zhang Chen: Our topic is marine conservation, climate change, and sustainable resources. The content should be serious and accurate, but if it’s too technical, I’m afraid the audience will lose interest.

Professor Chen: That’s a common concern. A good academic presentation should not be dry, but it also should not become entertainment without substance.

Zhang Chen: So is it acceptable to add humour, animations, or interactive elements?

Professor Chen: Yes, but only when they serve the message. Humour can reduce distance, visuals can clarify complex ideas, and interaction can involve the audience. But they should never distract from the scientific content.

Zhang Chen: We were thinking of using an interactive coral reef map or a short pollution simulation. Would that be too flashy?

Professor Chen: Not if you connect each tool to a learning point. A coral reef map can show biodiversity loss. A pollution simulation can show how human activity affects marine ecosystems.

Zhang Chen: So the problem is not whether we use creative elements, but whether they support the argument.

Professor Chen: Exactly. Ask yourself: Does this element help the audience understand, remember, or care about the issue? If yes, keep it. If it is only decorative, remove it.

Zhang Chen: I also worry about oversimplifying. If we make the presentation too accessible, maybe we lose technical accuracy.

Professor Chen: Accessibility does not mean removing evidence. Use clear language, but keep accurate terms, data, and sources. For example, when discussing coral bleaching, explain it simply, but still mention temperature stress, symbiotic algae, and ecosystem impact.

Zhang Chen: So we should make the explanation understandable, not shallow.

Professor Chen: Precisely. A strong presentation has three layers: accurate science, clear structure, and thoughtful delivery.

Zhang Chen: Then our structure could be: introduce the problem, show real data, explain the environmental impact, and present possible engineering or policy solutions.

Professor Chen: That would work well. You can also begin with one short audience question, such as which marine problem they think is most urgent, then use their responses to lead into your data.

Zhang Chen: That sounds better than adding random jokes. It involves the audience but keeps the topic serious.

Professor Chen: Exactly. Engagement does not have to mean entertainment. It can mean relevance, clarity, visual evidence, and meaningful audience involvement.

Zhang Chen: I understand now. We’re not choosing between professionalism and creativity. We’re using creativity to make professional content easier to understand.

Professor Chen: Well said. Make the presentation accurate enough for experts, but clear enough for non-specialists.

Zhang Chen: Thank you, Professor Chen. I’ll revise the slides with evidence first, and use creative elements only when they support the message.

Professor Chen: Good. Before finalizing the slides, ask one question about every visual or activity: What does this help the audience understand?

Zhang Chen: I will. This gives me a much clearer direction.

### Audio 2: Engineering Decision | Understanding Engineering Similarities and Differences

Wang Hao and Su Fei are comparing engineering in deep-sea, deep-Earth, and polar environments.

Wang Hao: Su Fei, I used to think deep-sea exploration and deep-Earth exploration were completely different. One goes down into the ocean, and the other goes down into the ground.

Su Fei: Same here. But after reading the two reports, I think they are similar engineering problems in many ways. In both cases, engineers send equipment into places humans cannot easily enter or repair.

Wang Hao: Right. The first shared challenge is pressure. A deep-sea glider like Haiyan must survive huge water pressure. A deep drilling system must survive high pressure from rock layers and underground fluids.

Su Fei: And both systems need strong structures and reliable materials. In the ocean, materials must resist corrosion from salt water. Underground, drilling tools face high temperature, strong vibration, friction, and wear. The environments are different, but the design question is similar: How can the system keep working when the environment attacks it?

Wang Hao: Energy is another issue. Haiyan has to travel for a long time with limited battery power, so it uses buoyancy changes instead of a propeller all the time. Deep-Earth drilling also needs stable energy supply to power drilling, sensing, cooling, and monitoring systems.

Su Fei: Data is also difficult. Underwater communication is limited, and satellite signals cannot reach deep water. Underground, signals are also hard to transmit through rock. So both projects need sensors, data processing, and smart ways to send useful information back.

Wang Hao: That makes me think of polar exploration. Is it the same kind of problem?

Su Fei: Partly yes. Polar exploration also happens in an extreme and remote environment. Equipment must survive low temperature, ice, snow, strong wind, limited energy supply, and difficult communication. If something breaks, repair may be slow or impossible.

Wang Hao: But the main threats are different. Deep-sea systems fight pressure and corrosion. Deep-Earth systems fight heat, pressure, vibration, and tool wear. Polar systems fight cold, ice movement, storms, and long periods of darkness.

Su Fei: Exactly. Polar exploration is often more accessible than the deep sea or deep Earth, because people can work on the surface. But it has stronger weather uncertainty and logistics problems. A polar research station, rover, or icebreaker depends heavily on transport, shelter, power, and emergency planning.

Wang Hao: So the common idea is not “ocean” or “underground” or “ice.” The common idea is designing reliable systems for places with high risk, limited access, and incomplete information.

Su Fei: And no single major can solve that. Mechanical engineers design structures and moving parts. Materials scientists choose strong and resistant materials. Automation engineers design control systems. Instrumentation specialists build sensors. Computer scientists process data. Environmental scientists assess impact. Safety engineers plan for failure.

Wang Hao: So extreme-environment engineering is really teamwork under pressure.

Su Fei: Yes, totally.

Audio 1: Engineering Conversations | Finding a Way Through an Energy Storage Course

Wang Hao asks Su Fei for help with an energy-storage course.
